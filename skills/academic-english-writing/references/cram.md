# Exam Sprint (期末速通)

Reference for learners facing a graded writing task in days or hours. The goal shifts from *learn to write* to *stop losing marks*.

---

## 1. What sprinting actually is

Cramming does not build writing ability in a week. What it can do is remove the losses that come from not knowing how the paper is scored: a vague thesis, paragraphs with no claim, evidence with no interpretation, a missing reference list, a conclusion that adds new ideas.

Realistic expectation, stated honestly to the learner: **a well-executed sprint moves a paper from the 60s–70s into the 80s. It does not produce a 90+ paper.** The 90s band requires an original angle and genuine engagement with sources, which no template supplies. Say this once, at the start, so the learner spends effort on the right things.

Priority order when time is short:

```
论点清楚 > 段落有主张 > 每处证据有解读 > 引用格式齐全 > 语言漂亮
```

Language is last, and it is where most learners spend most of their time. That trade is the single most common reason a structurally sound paper scores mediocre.

## 2. Time-tiered plans

### Seven days

| Day | Task | Output |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Read the rubric. Rewrite your last essay's thesis until it is contestable. | One-sentence thesis that a classmate could disagree with |
| 2 | Build a sentence outline for the target topic | 5–7 Roman-numeral lines, each a claim |
| 3 | Draft the body only, 500 words a day | Body complete, no introduction |
| 4 | Add evidence and one interpretation sentence per piece of evidence | Every paragraph has an *This suggests that* sentence |
| 5 | Write introduction and conclusion; leave the abstract last | Full draft |
| 6 | Format pass: citations, reference list, headings, spacing | Zero format errors |
| 7 | Timed rehearsal: write a 300-word version in 40 minutes | Speed and stamina check |

### Three days

| Day | Task |
|---|---|
| 1 | Learn the five template sentences (§3) and the rubric. Write one thesis plus outline. |
| 2 | Write the full draft. Do not stop to polish. Every paragraph gets a claim sentence and one interpreted piece of evidence. |
| 3 | Format pass, then the ten-item failure list (§5). Fix only what is on the list. |

### One day

| Block | Task |
|---|---|
| Morning (2h) | Memorize the five sentences. Write one thesis and one full body paragraph to prove you can execute them. |
| Afternoon (2h) | Draft the whole text fast. Leave `[??]` where stuck and keep going. |
| Evening (1.5h) | Replace `[??]`, add the introduction and conclusion, then run §5. |
| Last 30 min | Read the paper aloud once. Fix only what sounds broken. |

### Three hours

Do not attempt a full paper. Do this instead:

1. Write the thesis (5 min).
2. Write topic sentences for every paragraph (10 min).
3. Fill each paragraph with one piece of evidence and one interpretation (60 min).
4. Write the introduction's last sentence and the conclusion (20 min).
5. Format the reference list from whatever sources you actually used (20 min).
6. Run the five highest-value checks: thesis, topic sentences, interpretations, citations, word count (15 min).

## 3. The five sentences worth memorizing

These are not style models. They are structural insurance: each one forces a move that graders look for.

**① Thesis (position + reason)**
> *Although ________, this essay argues that ________, because ________.*

The concession makes the claim contestable; the *because* forces a reason preview.

**② Topic sentence (claim, not label)**
> *The most serious consequence of ________ is not ________ but ________.*

A label says *Another effect is X*. This says *X is the serious one, and here is the ranking* — a claim.

**③ Introducing evidence**
> *This pattern is documented in ________, where ________ (Author, Year).*

Naming what the source documents, rather than announcing that a source exists.

**④ Interpreting evidence**
> *This suggests that ________, at least under the conditions of ________.*

The most commonly missing sentence in undergraduate papers. The hedge keeps it defensible.

**⑤ Concluding**
> *If ________ holds, then ________ must be reconsidered.*

Ends on a consequence rather than a summary.

## 4. The one-page card

Print or copy this; it is what to re-read in the last thirty minutes.

**Before writing**
- [ ] Is my thesis a claim someone could disagree with?
- [ ] Does my thesis preview two or three reasons?
- [ ] Do I have a contestable counterargument to address?

**While writing**
- [ ] Every paragraph opens with a claim, not a fact or a citation
- [ ] Every piece of evidence is followed by one sentence of interpretation
- [ ] No paragraph has two centres
- [ ] Quotations are under 10% of the text

**After writing**
- [ ] Introduction: background → gap → thesis, in that order, within four moves
- [ ] Conclusion: no new evidence, no new source, no *In conclusion*
- [ ] In-text citations and reference entries match one for one
- [ ] References alphabetical, hanging indent, journal names italic
- [ ] Word count within ±10%
- [ ] No *very / really / a lot of / etc.*
- [ ] No sentence starts with a numeral
- [ ] No Chinese punctuation anywhere

**Tense**
- [ ] Method and results: past
- [ ] Interpretation and general claims: present
- [ ] Author claims: present

## 5. Ten failure points that cost the most, ranked

| # | Failure | Typical cost |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Thesis is a fact, a question, or missing | Fatal for the top two bands |
| 2 | Evidence with no interpretation | −1 to −2 per instance; caps the reasoning dimension at half |
| 3 | Paragraphs with no claim sentence | −2 each; makes the essay read as notes |
| 4 | No reference list, or list not matching citations | −5 plus a credibility hit |
| 5 | Conclusion introduces a new idea | −4; signals the essay was not planned |
| 6 | No counterargument in an argumentative prompt | −4 |
| 7 | Introduction spends a page on *With the development of society* | −3; the gap never arrives |
| 8 | Patchwritten paraphrase | Academic-integrity risk, not just a deduction |
| 9 | Quotation with no page number | −1 each, and it is the easiest thing to fix |
| 10 | Word count off by more than 20% | −3 |

Note that nine of the ten are structural. This is the argument for spending the last day on structure rather than vocabulary.

## 6. Exam-room sequence

For a timed in-class essay, 60 minutes:

| Minutes | Action |
|---|---|
| 0–3 | Read the prompt twice. Underline the instruction verb (discuss / evaluate / compare) and the scope words |
| 3–8 | Write the thesis and three topic sentences. Do not start writing prose yet |
| 8–45 | Write the body first, introduction second. If stuck, write the paragraph you know best |
| 45–52 | Write the conclusion |
| 52–57 | Run the four highest-value checks: thesis, topic sentences, interpretations, citations |
| 57–60 | Fix only comprehension-blocking errors |

The single most valuable habit: **spend five minutes on the outline before writing a word.** It feels like waste and it is not. Papers written without an outline drift in paragraph three, and drift is expensive to repair under time pressure.

---

## 7. What not to do

- Do not memorize whole essays. Prompts change and a memorized essay reads as a memorized essay.
- Do not learn a list of "advanced words". Misused vocabulary costs more than plain vocabulary.
- Do not spend the last day on grammar drills. Grammar is worth 25 points and structure is worth 45; both are movable, but structure moves faster.
- Do not write the abstract first. It is a summary of text that does not exist yet.
- Do not submit without reading the paper aloud once. It catches the errors that reading silently auto-corrects.
