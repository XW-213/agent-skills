# Paper Structure — Section Playbook

Reference for every section of an English academic paper. Each entry gives the function, the required moves, typical proportion, dominant tense, sentence patterns, and the errors that most often cost marks.

Assumes APA 7th edition. If the instructor specifies MLA or Chicago, keep the section logic and change only the citation apparatus.

---

## 0. Anatomy at a glance

| Section | Share of length | Dominant tense | One-line function |
|---|---|---|---|
| Title | — | — | Identify object and approach |
| Abstract | 150–250 words | Present + past | Self-contained condensation |
| Introduction | 10–15% | Present / present perfect | Why this is worth studying |
| Literature review | 15–25% | Present perfect / present | What is known, what is missing |
| Methodology | 10–15% | Past | How it was done, repeatably |
| Results | 15–20% | Past | What was found, without interpretation |
| Discussion | 20–25% | Past + present | What the findings mean |
| Conclusion | 5–10% | Present | So what, and how far it reaches |
| References | — | — | One-to-one correspondence |

For a short argumentative essay, collapse to: Introduction → Body paragraphs (3–5) → Counterargument and rebuttal → Conclusion.

## 1. Title

**Moves**
1. Name the object of study.
2. Name the angle, variable, or method.
3. Optional: signal the finding's direction without overclaiming.

**Form rules**
- 10–15 words, no full sentence, no terminal period.
- Colon structure is the most reliable: *Main claim or topic: qualifier or method*.
- Title case for the main words.
- No abbreviations that the reader cannot decode.
- Avoid *A Study of*, *An Analysis of*, *My View on* — these add words without adding information.

**Examples**
- Weak: *A Study on AI and Writing*
- Weak: *Some Thoughts about Feedback in English Classes*
- Strong: *Automated versus Peer Feedback in Chinese EFL Writing: A Quasi-Experimental Study*
- Strong: *Why Rubrics Fail: Assessing Writing in Large Undergraduate Classes*

## 2. Abstract

**Five-sentence formula**

| Slot | Content | Pattern |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Background or problem | *X has become increasingly central to Y, yet Z remains unclear.* |
| 2 | What this study did | *This study examines whether ... using ... with ... .* |
| 3 | Principal findings | *Results indicate that ... (specific numbers, direction, magnitude).* |
| 4 | Interpretation | *These findings suggest that ... .* |
| 5 | Implication or boundary | *The results are limited to ... and point to ... .* |

**Must not appear**
- Citations of any kind.
- Figure or table numbers.
- Undefined abbreviations.
- A table of contents in prose: *This paper first introduces X, then analyzes Y, and finally concludes Z.*
- Findings that do not appear in the body.

**Keywords**: 3–5 items drawn from title and abstract, using the field's established terms rather than invented phrases.

## 3. Introduction

**Funnel of four moves** (compatible with Swales' CARS model: establish territory → establish niche → occupy niche)

| Move | Content | Length |
|---|---|---|
| 1 Territory | Establish that the topic matters and is currently active | 1–3 sentences |
| 2 Background | What prior work has established, with attribution | 2–5 sentences |
| 3 Niche | The specific gap, tension, or unresolved question | 1–3 sentences |
| 4 Occupation | Purpose, approach, and thesis or research questions | 2–4 sentences |

**Patterns**

- Territory: *In recent years, X has attracted considerable attention in Y.* / *The rapid expansion of X has raised questions about Y.*
- Background: *A substantial body of research has examined ... (Author, Year; Author, Year).* / *Prior studies have generally found that ... .*
- Niche: *However, these studies have relied almost exclusively on self-reported data.* / *Little is known about how X operates under condition Y.* / *Findings on this point remain inconsistent.*
- Occupation: *This study therefore investigates whether ... .* / *Drawing on data from ..., this paper argues that ... .* / *The paper proceeds in four parts: ...* (use sparingly)

**Gap statements that survive scrutiny**
- Avoid *no one has ever studied X* — almost always false and easily checked.
- Prefer scope-based gaps: *research has concentrated on A, with comparatively little attention to B*.
- Prefer method-based gaps: *existing work is largely cross-sectional; longitudinal evidence is scarce*.
- Prefer population-based gaps: *these findings derive from native-speaker samples and may not transfer to L2 writers*.

**Errors**
- Two pages of global context before the topic appears.
- Thesis absent or buried; the reader cannot state your claim after reading.
- Arguing in the introduction. The introduction justifies the study; the body argues it.
- Announcing the structure instead of stating a position.

## 4. Literature review

**Purpose**: not to summarize everything read, but to construct the specific conversation into which your study enters.

**Three organizational options — pick one and sustain it**

| Pattern | Logic | Use when |
|---|---|---|
| Thematic | 3–4 issue clusters, each reviewing several sources | Default choice |
| Chronological | Early work → recent work, showing conceptual evolution | The idea itself has a history |
| Methodological | Quantitative / qualitative / mixed strands | The field argues about method |

**Paragraph-level cycle** (repeat per cluster)
1. Synthesize: *Several studies report that ... (A, Year; B, Year).*
2. Compare: *These findings converge on X, although B (Year) reports the opposite under condition Z.*
3. Evaluate: *The discrepancy may stem from differing measures of ... .*
4. Relate: *Neither strand, however, examines ... .*

**Requirements**
- Every cluster ends by pointing at the gap the present study fills.
- The final paragraph must close the review explicitly: *Taken together, these studies establish A, but leave B unresolved. This study addresses B by ... .*
- Conflicting evidence is reported, not smoothed away.
- Citation stacks must be differentiated: instead of *Many studies have shown this (A, 2019; B, 2020)*, state what each contributed.

**Errors**
- Annotated-bibliography drift: one paragraph per source, no cross-reference between them.
- Cherry-picking only supportive work.
- Reviewing sources that never reappear in the discussion.
- No gap statement, so the paper's existence is unmotivated.

## 5. Methodology

**Objective**: enable replication. If a reader cannot repeat the study from this section, it has failed regardless of prose quality.

**Four subsections**

| Subsection | Must state |
|---|---|
| Participants / materials | N, sampling method, relevant characteristics, recruitment, consent |
| Instruments | Tests, questionnaires, corpora, software; source and reliability/validity evidence |
| Procedure | Chronological steps with durations |
| Analysis | Statistical tests or coding scheme, and the reason for choosing them |

**Patterns**
- *Participants were 84 second-year undergraduates (M age = 19.4, SD = 0.9) enrolled in ... .*
- *The instrument was adapted from X (Year); internal consistency was acceptable (α = .87).*
- *Data were collected in three sessions over four weeks.*
- *Responses were coded independently by two raters; inter-rater reliability was κ = .81.*
- *Differences between groups were tested with an independent-samples t test, with α set at .05.*

**Errors**
- Narrative of the researcher's actions (*I opened the software and ran the test*) instead of a description of the design.
- Sample size with no justification and no power consideration.
- Method chosen with no rationale; the reader cannot tell why interviews were not used.
- Tense drift into the present.

## 6. Results

**Rule: report, do not interpret.** Interpretation belongs to the discussion.

**Moves**
1. Restate each research question briefly, in order.
2. Report the corresponding result with the relevant statistic.
3. Point to the table or figure that carries the detail.

**Patterns**
- *As Table 1 shows, the experimental group outperformed the comparison group on post-test accuracy.*
- *The difference was statistically significant, t(82) = 2.31, p = .023, d = 0.66.*
- *No significant difference emerged for fluency, p = .41.*
- *Qualitative analysis yielded three themes: ... .*

**Conventions**
- Report p values to three decimals without a leading zero; report effect sizes alongside significance.
- Do not repeat every cell of a table in prose; highlight the values that carry the argument.
- Every figure and table needs a number, a title, and a note defining abbreviations and units.
- Negative and null results are reported, not omitted.

**Errors**
- Discussion smuggled into results (*this proves that AI feedback is superior*).
- Tables with no in-text reference.
- Only p values, no effect size, no descriptive statistics.

## 7. Discussion

**Four moves, in order**

| Move | Content |
|---|---|
| 1 Restate | Answer each research question in one or two sentences |
| 2 Explain | Account for the pattern — mechanism, plausible cause, alternative reading |
| 3 Dialogue | Compare with prior findings: agreement, disagreement, reconciliation |
| 4 Bound | Limitations as boundary conditions, plus next steps |

**Patterns**
- *The present findings indicate that ... .*
- *One explanation for this pattern is that ... .*
- *This result is consistent with Chen (2018), who found ... .*
- *It diverges, however, from Li (2020), possibly because ... .*
- *These results should be interpreted in light of three limitations: ... .*
- *Future work should test whether ... .*

**Errors**
- A second results section with no explanation added.
- Overgeneralization: 60 participants from one university, conclusions about "Chinese students" as a whole.
- Limitations written as self-flagellation rather than as scope definition.
- Introducing entirely new literature here that should have been in the review.

---

## 8. Conclusion

**Moves**
1. Restate the contribution, reworded, not copied from the introduction.
2. State the theoretical or practical implication.
3. Close with a sentence that carries weight: a caution, an application, or an open question.

**Rules**
- No new evidence, no new citations (a single illustrative citation is acceptable in some fields; check the discipline).
- Do not begin with *In conclusion*. The heading already says so.
- Do not merely list what each paragraph said.
- Signal the boundary: under what conditions does the claim hold?

**Errors**
- Conclusion longer than the discussion.
- Restating the introduction verbatim.
- A closing sentence with no content: *This topic deserves further study.*

---

## 9. References

- One-to-one correspondence with in-text citations. A missing entry or an uncited entry is an error of the same severity.
- Alphabetical by first author surname; hanging indent; DOI as a full URL.
- Consistent capitalization: sentence case for article and book titles, title case for journal names.
- See `citations.md` for every source type.

## 10. Consistency check: the coherence audit

Run after drafting, before editing language.

1. Extract the thesis. Write it on one line.
2. Extract every topic sentence. List them in order.
3. Read the list alone. Does it form a compressed version of the argument, with no orphan and no repetition?
4. For each topic sentence, locate the evidence that supports it. If none, the paragraph is assertion.
5. For each piece of evidence, locate the sentence that interprets it. If none, the paragraph is a digest.
6. Check that the conclusion's claim does not exceed what the evidence supports. If it does, either weaken the claim or add evidence.
7. Check the counterargument, if the genre requires one: is the strongest opposing view represented, or a straw man?

Report the result of this audit as a short list of structural defects, ordered by severity. Structure defects outrank language defects and should be fixed first.
