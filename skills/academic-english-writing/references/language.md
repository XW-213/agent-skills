# Academic Language and Stance

Reference for register, hedging, attribution verbs, tense, and sentence-level revision, with a dedicated section on errors characteristic of Chinese-L1 writers.

---

## 1. Three governing principles

| Principle | Test question | Failure symptom |
|---|---|---|
| Objectivity | Would this sentence survive if all evaluative adjectives were deleted? | Argument carried by *very serious*, *extremely important* |
| Precision | Are the numbers, range, and conditions stated? | *many*, *a lot*, *significantly better* with no figure |
| Restraint | Does the conclusion exceed the evidence? | *proves*, *undeniably*, *all students* from a small sample |

Academic prose earns authority by narrowing claims to what evidence can carry. Vagueness is not caution; it is absence of information.

## 2. Register: formality substitutions

| Informal / L1-influenced | Academic |
|---|---|
| a lot of, lots of, plenty of | numerous, considerable, a substantial number of |
| very important, very big | crucial, critical, substantial, pronounced |
| get, got | obtain, acquire, receive, experience |
| show | demonstrate, indicate, reveal, illustrate |
| think, believe, feel | argue, maintain, contend, hold, posit |
| but | however, nevertheless, yet, although |
| so | therefore, consequently, thus, accordingly |
| and so on, etc. | delete, or specify: *such as A, B, and C* |
| things, stuff, something | factors, elements, aspects, conditions (then name them) |
| nowadays | currently, in recent decades, at present |
| I think, in my opinion | It is argued that / The evidence suggests that |
| more and more | an increasing number of, growing |
| because of this | for this reason, accordingly, as a result |
| find out | determine, establish, identify |
| talk about | address, discuss, examine |
| good / bad | effective / ineffective, beneficial / detrimental |
| a research | a study, an investigation (research is uncountable) |

**Warning against mechanical substitution.** Replacing every *important* with *crucial* produces text that reads as a thesaurus exercise. Academic texture comes from information density — specific objects, conditions, quantities — not from Latinate vocabulary. If a sentence still says nothing after upgrading its words, the problem is content.

**Contractions and first person**
- No contractions: *don't*, *can't*, *it's* → *do not*, *cannot*, *it is*.
- APA 7 permits *I* and *we*; use *we* for multi-author work, *I* for single-author reflective passages. Never *you* in expository prose.
- *One* as a subject is grammatically safe but reads stiff; prefer recasting in the passive or naming the agent.

## 3. Hedging: calibrate to evidence

| Strength | Expressions | Use when |
|---|---|---|
| Near-assertion | demonstrates, establishes, proves, shows conclusively | Replicated experimental evidence, large effect |
| Strong | shows, indicates, confirms, reveals | Clear, consistent results |
| Moderate (default) | suggests, points to, is consistent with, appears to | Single study, observational design |
| Weak | may, might, could, is likely to | Mechanistic speculation, small sample |
| Minimal commitment | it is possible that, one might speculate, there is some indication | Exploratory or contested terrain |

**Softening modifiers available for attachment**: *likely, presumably, largely, in most cases, to some extent, under these conditions, among this population, arguably, relatively*.

**The two opposite failures**

- **Overclaiming**: *These results prove that automated feedback should replace teachers.* → *These results suggest that automated feedback can reduce the time teachers spend on routine error correction.*
- **Over-hedging**: *It may possibly be suggested that there might perhaps be a certain tendency...* One hedge per claim. Stacking hedges reads as an absence of judgement, which is also a form of imprecision.

**Rule of thumb**: hedge the generalization, not the observation. *The experimental group scored higher* is a fact about the data and needs no hedge; *automated feedback is more effective* is a generalization and does.

## 4. Reporting verbs: attribution as stance

The verb chosen tells the reader how much confidence to place in the cited claim, and how you position yourself toward it.

| Stance | Verbs | Typical pattern |
|---|---|---|
| Neutral, descriptive | state, report, note, describe, observe, explain, outline | *Wang (2021) reports that ...* |
| Tentative | suggest, indicate, imply, propose, hypothesize, speculate | *The data suggest that ...* |
| Strong, assertive | argue, contend, demonstrate, establish, confirm, assert, maintain | *Li (2020) demonstrates that ...* |
| Critical | challenge, question, dispute, refute, overlook, overstate, neglect | *This account overlooks the role of ...* |
| Supportive | support, corroborate, be consistent with, extend | *These findings corroborate those of Chen (2018).* |

**Verb choice errors**
- *mention* used for a central finding: it implies the source said it in passing, which misrepresents the source and weakens your own evidence.
- *say* used for written argument: acceptable only for direct speech.
- *state* used throughout: monotonous and stance-free; the reader cannot tell whether you agree.
- *prove* applied to a study that reported a correlation.

**Grammar patterns to keep straight**
- verb + *that* clause: *argues that X is ...*
- verb + noun phrase: *examines the relationship between ...*
- verb + *-ing*: *recommends investing in ...*, *suggests reallocating ...*
- verb + object + infinitive: *prompts researchers to reconsider ...*

## 5. Tense rules by section

| Situation | Tense | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Established fact, general principle | Present | *Retrieval practice improves long-term retention.* |
| Current state of the field | Present / present perfect | *Research has shifted toward ... .* |
| What a specific study did | Past | *Smith (2019) collected data from 120 students.* |
| What an author argues | Present | *Smith (2019) argues that ... .* |
| Your own procedure | Past | *Participants were assigned to two conditions.* |
| Your own results | Past | *The experimental group outperformed the comparison group.* |
| Interpretation, implication | Present | *This suggests that ... .* |
| Reference to a table or figure | Present | *Table 1 shows ... .* |
| Future work | Present / modal | *Future studies should examine ... .* |

Mnemonic: **actions past, truths present, authors always present.**

Tense inconsistency within a paragraph is one of the most visible markers of non-native academic prose. When editing, check the verb of every sentence in a results paragraph for accidental drift into the present.

## 6. Sentence-level tools

**Nominalization** — turn a clause into a noun phrase to pack information and to foreground the abstract process.
- *People use this method widely.* → *The widespread adoption of this method ...*
- *Students improved a lot.* → *The magnitude of student improvement ...*
- Do not nominalize everything: strings of abstract nouns (*the implementation of the evaluation of the assessment of...*) become unreadable. Two nominalizations per sentence is already a lot.

**Passive voice** — use when the agent is unknown, irrelevant, or obvious from context; in methods, either voice is acceptable in APA 7, and the active is often clearer.
- Good: *The samples were stored at −20 °C.*
- Bad: *It was found by the researchers that the results were significant.* (empty agent, padded clause)

**Information structure** — put known information at the start of the sentence and new information at the end. This single habit fixes most cohesion problems without adding connectives.

**Cohesion devices, ranked by strength**
1. Lexical repetition of the key term (strongest, most underused)
2. Pronoun or demonstrative reference (*this pattern*, *such evidence*)
3. Connective (*however*, *therefore*, *in contrast*)
4. Synonym substitution (weakest, and risky in technical writing)

Do not decorate with connectives to simulate logic. If two sentences do not actually stand in a contrast relation, *however* will not create one.

**Connective precision**

| Connective | Actual meaning | Common misuse |
|---|---|---|
| however | contrast with immediately preceding claim | used as a general filler at sentence start |
| on the contrary | the previous statement is false; the opposite holds | used where *by contrast* is meant |
| by contrast / on the other hand | parallel comparison of two cases | confused with *on the contrary* |
| therefore / thus | logical consequence of the preceding claim | used where only temporal sequence holds |
| moreover / furthermore | addition of a further point in the same direction | used to introduce a counterpoint |
| nevertheless | concession survives | used as a synonym for *however* |
| while / whereas | simultaneous contrast within one sentence | *while* used for *although* ambiguity |

## 7. Errors characteristic of Chinese-L1 writers

Work through this table when editing. Each entry gives the symptom and the repair.

| # | Error | Symptom | Repair |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Run-on sentence | Four or more clauses joined by commas | Split; use a semicolon for closely related clauses; subordinate with *which*, *because*, *although* |
| 2 | Comma splice | *It is important, we must act.* | Period, semicolon, or coordinating conjunction |
| 3 | *There be* overuse | *There are many students think that ...* | *Many students think that ...* |
| 4 | Article omission or insertion | *In modern society, government should ...* | Singular countable nouns need a determiner: *the government*, *governments*; abstract nouns usually take none: *education*, *research* |
| 5 | Subject–verb agreement | *The number of students are increasing.* | *The number ... is* (head noun is *number*); *A number of students are* |
| 6 | Wrong part of speech | *This research is very success.* | *successful* (adj.), *succeed* (v.), *success* (n.) |
| 7 | Misplaced modifier | *AI can help students effectively improve writing.* | Place the modifier next to what it modifies, or recast: *AI can effectively help students improve their writing.* |
| 8 | Countability | *many informations*, *a research*, *equipments* | *much information*, *a study*, *equipment* |
| 9 | Present tense for completed studies | *Wang (2019) collects data ...* | *collected* — the procedure is past; the argument is present |
| 10 | Redundant synonym triplets | *important and significant and crucial* | Keep the single most accurate word |
| 11 | Undefined abbreviation at first use | *AWE was used ...* | *automated writing evaluation (AWE)* at first mention |
| 12 | Overuse of *we/you* in expository text | *You can see that ...* | *It can be seen that ...* / name the agent |
| 13 | Chinese-style topic–comment order | *This problem, many scholars have studied it.* | *Many scholars have studied this problem.* |
| 14 | *etc.* and *and so on* as argument closure | *...such as accuracy, fluency, etc.* | Close the list explicitly: *...such as accuracy and fluency* |
| 15 | Numerical inconsistency | *8 participants* mid-sentence | APA: spell out numbers below 10, use numerals for 10 and above; never begin a sentence with a numeral |

## 8. Punctuation and mechanics (APA)

- Serial comma before *and* in a list of three or more: *accuracy, fluency, and complexity*.
- Quotation marks are curly; the period goes inside the closing quotation mark in US style.
- Em dash — no spaces on either side. En dash – for ranges (*pp. 45–52*).
- Hyphenate compound modifiers before a noun (*well-designed study*) but not after (*the study was well designed*).
- *e.g.* introduces an example; *i.e.* restates. Both take a comma in US style.
- No space before a colon in English; one space after.
- Avoid Chinese full-width punctuation entirely in English text; this includes 、，。：；（）“”.
- Keep one space after sentence-final punctuation, not two.

## 9. Revision procedure for language

1. Read the paragraph aloud. Anywhere you run out of breath or lose the thread marks a sentence boundary problem.
2. Identify the grammatical subject of each sentence. If the subjects jump between abstract nouns and *it/there*, rewrite for a consistent topic chain.
3. Check every verb for tense against the table in §5.
4. Delete the first clause of any sentence that begins with a scene-setting phrase which adds no information (*With the development of society, ...*).
5. Replace every evaluative adjective with a fact, or delete it.
6. Confirm each connective expresses the relation it claims.
7. Read backwards, sentence by sentence, from the last sentence. This breaks the prose flow and exposes agreement, article, and spelling errors that forward reading hides.
