# Citation, Attribution, and Plagiarism

Reference for APA 7th edition in-text citation, reference-list construction, the three modes of source integration, and the plagiarism decision table.

---

## 1. In-text citation forms

| Case | Parenthetical | Narrative |
|---|---|---|
| One author | *(Wang, 2021)* | *Wang (2021) argues that ...* |
| Two authors | *(Wang & Li, 2021)* | *Wang and Li (2021) argue that ...* |
| Three or more | *(Wang et al., 2021)* | *Wang et al. (2021) argue that ...* |
| Group author, first mention | *(World Health Organization [WHO], 2020)* | *The World Health Organization (WHO, 2020) reports ...* |
| Group author, later mentions | *(WHO, 2020)* | *WHO (2020) reports ...* |
| No date | *(Wang, n.d.)* | *Wang (n.d.)* |
| Direct quotation | *(Wang, 2021, p. 45)* | *Wang (2021) states that “...” (p. 45).* |
| Multiple sources | Alphabetical, semicolon-separated: *(Li, 2018; Wang, 2021)* | — |
| Same author, same year | *(Wang, 2021a, 2021b)* | *Wang (2021a) ...* |
| Secondary citation | *(Original, 1999, as cited in Wang, 2021)* — list only Wang in the references | *Original (1999, as cited in Wang, 2021) ...* |
| Whole work | *(Wang, 2021)* or with location *(Wang, 2021, pp. 45–52)* | — |

**Rules that are graded**
- Every sentence containing an idea from a source carries attribution. One citation at the end of a five-sentence passage is insufficient — the reader cannot tell where the source's content stops.
- *et al.* takes no period after *et* and no italics.
- In the reference list, use *&*; in narrative text, use *and*.
- Page numbers are required for direct quotations, and for paraphrases of a specific passage where the reader would benefit from locating it.
- Never cite a source you have not read. If the reading was second-hand, use the *as cited in* form.

## 2. Reference list by source type (APA 7)

**Journal article**
> Wang, L., & Li, M. (2021). Automated feedback in EFL writing: A meta-analysis. *System, 98*, 102–118. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.system.2021.102118

**Journal article, 21+ authors** — list the first 19, then an ellipsis, then the final author.

**Book**
> Zemach, D. E., Broudy, D., & Valvona, C. (2011). *Writing research papers: From essay to research paper*. Macmillan Education.

**Edited book chapter**
> Chen, H. (2020). Peer response in Chinese classrooms. In K. Hyland (Ed.), *Second language writing* (pp. 145–163). Routledge.

**Doctoral dissertation or master's thesis**
> Zhao, Y. (2019). *The effects of automated writing evaluation on revision behavior* [Doctoral dissertation, Peking University]. CNKI.

**Report by an organization**
> World Health Organization. (2020). *Air pollution and health* (Report No. WHO/XX/2020.03). https://www.who.int/...

**Webpage**
> World Health Organization. (2020, June 3). *Air pollution and health*. https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/air-pollution

**Conference paper**
> Sun, Q. (2022, May 12–14). *Rubric design for large classes* [Paper presentation]. Symposium on Language Assessment, Beijing, China.

**Formatting**
- Hanging indent of 0.5 inch on every entry.
- Alphabetical by first author's surname, then by year.
- *Retrieved from* is no longer used; give the URL directly.
- DOI rendered as a complete `https://doi.org/...` link, no period at the end.
- Article titles and book titles in sentence case. Journal names and book titles italicized. Volume italicized, issue in parentheses and not italicized.
- Author names: surname first, initials with periods, commas between authors, *&* before the last.

## 3. Three modes of integration

| Mode | Use when | Requirements |
|---|---|---|
| **Quotation** | The exact wording is the object of analysis; a canonical definition; a phrase that cannot be improved | Quotation marks, author, year, page number. Under 10% of total length. Block format only for 40+ words |
| **Paraphrase** | The specific reasoning, data, or mechanism matters and must be preserved in detail | Change syntactic structure, not just vocabulary. Similar length to the original. Always cited |
| **Summary** | The gist of a whole article or section is needed | Substantial compression, own structure, always cited |

**The sandwich constraint**: every integrated source sits between your claim and your interpretation. Evidence in a paragraph with no surrounding analysis turns the paragraph into a digest and forfeits the marks for argument.

## 4. Paraphrase procedure (and the patchwriting trap)

1. Read until you can state the idea without looking.
2. Close the source and write the idea from memory in your own sentence structure.
3. Reopen the source and check three things: the meaning is intact; no six-word run is identical; the sentence architecture is not a mirror of the original.
4. Add the citation.

**Illustration**

Source: *Because automated feedback systems are trained on corpora drawn largely from native-speaker essays, their error detection tends to privilege native norms over legitimate L2 variation.* (Smith & Lee, 2019, p. 214)

- **Patchwriting (unacceptable)**: *Since automated feedback systems are trained on corpora taken mostly from native-speaker essays, their error detection tends to favor native norms over legitimate L2 variation.*
  Only four lexical items changed; clause order and structure are preserved.
- **Acceptable paraphrase**: *Smith and Lee (2019) argue that automated feedback tools are trained mainly on writing by native speakers, so these systems may treat acceptable second-language usage as error.*

**Structural moves that produce a real paraphrase**
- Swap the causal direction: result first, cause second.
- Convert a subordinate clause into a main clause, or the reverse.
- Nominalize a verb phrase, or verbalize a noun phrase.
- Change the subject: from the system to the writers, from the process to the outcome.
- Split one long sentence into two, or merge two into one.

**Paraphrase is not a thesaurus operation.** Replacing *tends to* with *is inclined to* and *privilege* with *favor* is still plagiarism, because the intellectual structure remains borrowed.

## 5. Plagiarism decision table

| Behaviour | Verdict | Note |
|---|---|---|
| Copying text, no quotation marks, citation at paragraph end | Plagiarism | The citation does not license verbatim copying |
| Copying text with quotation marks but no page number | Citation error, often penalized as plagiarism | Page required for quotations |
| Paraphrase with structural mirroring and synonym substitution | Plagiarism (patchwriting) | The most common undergraduate case |
| Paraphrase with proper citation | Acceptable | — |
| Idea used without any citation | Plagiarism | Ideas are owned even when words are not |
| In-text citation present, reference entry missing | Citation error | Verify one-to-one correspondence |
| Reference entry present, never cited in text | Citation error | Delete or cite it |
| Reusing one's own prior submitted work | Self-plagiarism in most institutions | Disclose and cite; check the course policy |
| Citing a source not actually read | Academic misconduct | Use *as cited in* |
| Fabricated reference or fabricated data | Serious misconduct | Never acceptable under any circumstance |

## 6. Citation density and placement

- Two to four attributions per body paragraph is typical. More suggests the paragraph is a literature digest; fewer suggests unsupported assertion.
- Do not open a paragraph with a quotation. State your claim first.
- Do not close a paragraph with a quotation. Close with your interpretation.
- Vary the placement: some attributions at the start of the sentence (narrative), some at the end (parenthetical). All-narrative reads as a book report; all-parenthetical hides your engagement.
- Long quotations are rarely appropriate in undergraduate papers. Prefer paraphrase plus a short embedded phrase: *Smith and Lee describe this as a form of “normative capture” (2019, p. 214).*
